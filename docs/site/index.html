Physics decides.

SweepCF is a humanoid traversal dataset in which every episode has a twin. One journey, one obstacle, two motions — and a physics simulator, not an annotator, says which one survives.

It exists to teach a policy why a motion had to change, which is the one thing a corpus of merely-varied rooms cannot.

The idea

Scene variation is not scene-conditioned behaviour.

Generate a thousand rooms and a policy learns that rooms vary. It does not learn which part of its body the room binds, or what to do about it.

What a normal corpus gives

Diverse geometry, each traversal labelled succeeded or failed. The supervision says succeeded here — never succeeded because it ducked.

What a matched pair gives

A second episode identical in every respect except the single edit the geometry forced. The difference in outcome has exactly one cause, so it can be attributed.

Who decides

The simulator. Contact forces in newtons, joint-level tracking, swept-volume clearance — not a human judging a video, and not a heuristic.

The tail is the honest part. Crouch, arms and side-step are the behaviours a traversal dataset most needs and the generator produces least — 18 of 150. That imbalance is why the corpus is built by editing motions rather than waiting for a generator to sample them.

Quality

One success rate would hide everything that matters.

Synthetic humanoid corpora usually report a single number. It conflates four different questions with four different answers, and the honest headline is the smallest of them.

LabelQuestionMeasured
embodiment_feasibleCan the body reach this pose at all?144 / 150 · 96%
controller_trackableCan the frozen controller execute it?96%
semantically_validDoes the execution contain the named behaviour?32%
scene_compatibleDoes it survive this room?the counterfactual signal itself

Commanded is not delivered

We ask for the smallest edit that clears each obstacle, then measure what actually arrives at the surface the obstacle binds against. It is consistently less, and by how much depends on the body part — a property of the controller, measured, not assumed.

EditSurvives executionCosts forward progress
Arms (tuck)88–105%falls as the edit grows
Legs (crouch)46–67%rises with depth

An adaptation that moves the arms is nearly free to the controller; one that moves the legs is charged for. This is a controller-characterisation result that holds whether or not you adopt our dataset thesis, and it is what makes per-episode fidelity columns necessary rather than decorative.

Why it helps

What this is good for.

Perceptive traversal

Learning which whole-body adjustment a doorway, shelf or low ceiling demands — from ego RGB or depth — rather than only whether a gap is passable. The matched pair supplies the contrast that makes the mapping learnable.

Navigation that respects a body

Planners treat the robot as a cylinder. Here the binding body part is recorded per episode, so a route can be costed by what it would bind — wrist at waist height, shoulder at chest, torso overhead.

Benchmarking, not just training

Thirty evaluation scenes frozen before any operator existed, with a geometry rule stating which can grade a policy at all. Test labels come from physics, so a benchmark cannot drift with an annotator.

Sim-to-real grounding

Every episode records the exact token stream the deployment runtime consumes, byte-identical to what the on-robot stack would see. The corpus is addressed to a real controller, not an abstraction of one.

Named honestly: we call the task perceptive whole-body traversal. It is not navigation, and nothing here claims continuous visuomotor control.

Where the work actually stands.

Everything above claim 5 is instrumentation and physics, and it is solid. The paper stands or falls on claim 5, which runs on a pre-registered date — 24 August — against whatever families exist then, because the question needs contrast rather than corpus size.

Known limits, stated rather than buried. Two verified families is a small n. The corpus is single-embodiment. No hardware replication yet. The human-review pass over 100 episodes is running and is submission-blocking. A prediction register records every refuted hypothesis and every retraction, including a same-day retraction of a scoring error that had made the family count look worse than it was.
