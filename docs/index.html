Training scenes built from what the robot actually did

A Unitree G1 walking down a corridor has two options at an overhead beam: keep walking, or request a crouch. Teaching it to choose needs encounters where the choice matters. Those encounters turn out to be geometrically scarce, and we can measure exactly how scarce.

Both experiments complete 684 physics executions 3 carriers · 12 held-out layouts Simulation only · no hardware claim

The finding

A training scene helps only if it contains a decision-relevant contrast: a placement where walking fails and the crouch succeeds. Build scenes that way and a fixed selector learns when to adapt. Build them any other way and it never adapts at all. Whether the contrast is proposed by a learned generator or an analytic solver makes no measurable difference.

All four arms were trained on exactly fifteen labelled encounter groups, from the same carriers, with the same frozen controller, the same 214-input selector and the same optimizer. The only thing that differs is how the training scenes were placed.

How it works

Four stages, each one falsifiable by the next

  1. Execute the contrast, don't imagine itIn an empty room the robot runs both commands: walk through, and request the crouch at 0.30 s. We keep the achieved 50 Hz body trajectories, not the reference the controller was asked to track.
  2. Place the beam where the two executions differAny placement the crouch clears by 10 mm and the walk strikes by 10 mm is a candidate. Four constructions compete: uniform, an analytic capsule-box solver, a learned generator, and a target-only ablation that never looks at the walk.
  3. Let physics decide the labelBoth commands are executed again with the beam present in Isaac Lab. A group is useful only if walking measurably fails and the crouch passes contact-qualified, under 1 N through the crossing with 0.3 s of upright stability.
  4. Train one fixed selector and test it elsewhereA deterministic linear model reads 144 ray values plus proprioception and predicts whether each command will succeed. It is evaluated on twelve layouts it never saw, on all three carriers.

What almost sank it

The first run of this experiment produced nothing from the learned generator: zero accepted scenes out of forty-eight proposals, and one from the analytic solver. It would have been easy to report that as a failure of the generator. It was not.

The acceptance test had been inherited from an earlier study that certified obstacle placement. It required each contrast to survive the beam moving ±20 mm. The entire executed window between a walk and a crouch is only 20 to 27 mm wide, so the test was asking a scene to tolerate a displacement the size of the effect itself.

The second contract was registered with its predictions filed before any proposal was drawn, changes exactly one thing, and keeps the original envelope as a reported diagnostic that gates nothing. A unit test asserts both contracts decide identically whenever their geometry agrees. The first study keeps its own results unchanged.

The thirty-six conditions are twelve layouts on three carriers, so those p values overstate independence. The claim that survives grouping is simpler: contrast construction wins on three carriers out of three, with no reverse case, and the learned-versus-analytic difference is a null at n = 3 rather than an equivalence.

One place the learned generator is measurably worse

Its training loss is 0.139 against the analytic solver's 0.00054 on identical architecture and label count. The reason is in the construction record: only 24 of its 34 critical scenes are visible to the robot's sensor at the moment the command must be issued, against 44 of 47 for the analytic solver. A contrast the robot cannot see is a label the learner cannot fit. That is a specific, fixable defect, and no visibility filter was added after the fact to hide it.

What this is not

Every result here is simulation. One beam family, two commands, ideal ray sensing, three development carriers, one frozen controller, and layouts held out rather than source ancestry. The evaluation demonstrates unseen-layout transfer, not source-held-out transfer, and nothing on this page may be read as uncertainty-robust: acceptance was judged at the exact pose the simulator authors.

A hand-written ray rule still reaches 46 of 72 conditions in the larger panel, above every learned arm, while failing to classify a single blocked scene as impassable where every learned selector classifies all six. Neither approach dominates, and we report both directions.

Toward the real robot

Five gates, in dependency order. Each has a measured starting point from this study.

1 · Re-price the contrast for a real obstacle pose

Because on hardware the beam's pose is uncertain, and this study deliberately assumed it is not.

The envelope sweep already states the cost: at ±20 mm only 2, 0 and 32 placements survive per carrier. A deployment needs either a much larger executed window, obtained by a deeper or slower adaptation, or obstacle localisation tight enough to make the nominal contract honest. Measure the achievable window against a real perception stack before building anything else.

have: the full envelope/yield curve

2 · Replace ideal rays with the real sensor

Because the selector's inputs are currently 144 exact ray returns from a scripted lidar.

Re-acquire the same encounters through the deployed depth or lidar pipeline, with its actual noise, dropout and field of view, and refit the same fixed selector. The visibility gap already found in simulation, 24 of 34 learned scenes observable against 44 of 47 analytic, is the quantity that will get worse first. The 500 ms observation-delay failure already measured is the timing budget this stage must respect.

have: delay panel, visibility per construction

3 · Source-held-out transfer

Because every result so far varies layout, not motion ancestry.

Eight candidate source identifiers are reserved against a provenance ledger and none has been generated or qualified. Acquire them, qualify their switching banks under the same 0.30 s contract, and re-run the four-arm comparison with ancestry held out. This is the single largest gap between the present claim and a general one, and it needs no new method.

have: reserved IDs, qualification protocol

4 · Close the loop the controller actually runs

Because the selector issues one command from a shared approach and never revises it.

A deployed robot decides repeatedly as the scene resolves. Extending to sequential decisions changes the state distribution the selector sees, so it needs matched data aggregation across every arm and fresh labels, not a re-scored version of these. The timing result, where 0.20 s and 0.40 s both fail while 0.30 s passes, shows how narrow the admissible window already is.

have: timing window, legal-transition manager

5 · Hardware safety qualification

Because a refusal here continues walking; it is not a stop.

Nothing in this study is a protective action. Before any physical trial the command bank needs a genuine abort, the blocked-scene classification needs to drive it, and contact thresholds calibrated in simulation need re-derivation against real contact sensing. Treat the 1 N contact-qualified criterion as a simulation convention, not a safety margin.

have: blocked-scene refusal at 6/6 for every learner

Reproducing it

The two studies together cost 6.6 contended GPU-hours on a single RTX 5080. Construction and fitting are CPU-only and take minutes; the cost is entirely Isaac Lab execution at roughly 32 seconds per cell.

Motion2Scene is part of GR00T-WholeBodyControl. The motion-tracking controller is SONIC, inherited and frozen; its locomotion capability is not a contribution of this work. Simulation results only, September 2026.
